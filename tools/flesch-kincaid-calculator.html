What the scores mean

The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level is the most widely used English readability formula. Its scores map to US school grades from sentence length and word complexity (Readable). Text for the general public should aim near grade 8, roughly schooling age 13 to 14.

The Flesch Reading Ease scale runs from 1 to 100, and 100 is the most readable. A score between 70 and 80 matches school grade 8 (Readable).

The Gunning fog index estimates the years of formal education needed to understand a text on first reading. A fog index of 12 corresponds to a US high school senior (Wikipedia). Text for a wide audience generally needs a fog index under 12. Near-universal understanding needs one under 8.

How we know the numbers are right

Every result here is recomputed in browser code from the text you paste, never guessed and never fetched. The tool counts sentences with terminal punctuation, splits words on whitespace, and estimates syllables with the standard vowel-group heuristic.

The history is short. Rudolf Flesch and J. Peter Kincaid built the grade-level formula in the 1970s with the US Navy, which used it for technical training manuals (Readable). Robert Gunning, a publisher turned consultant, introduced his fog index in 1952 (Wikipedia).

The formulas, stated openly

Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level = 0.39 × (words ÷ sentences) + 11.8 × (syllables ÷ words) − 15.59. The syllable count uses a vowel-group heuristic, so it is an estimate.

Flesch Reading Ease = 206.835 − 1.015 × (words ÷ sentences) − 84.6 × (syllables ÷ words) (Readable).

Gunning fog index = 0.4 × [(words ÷ sentences) + 100 × (complex words ÷ words)], where complex words have three or more syllables (Wikipedia).

Where the syllable heuristic breaks

Every browser calculator counts syllables with a vowel-group heuristic, because a real dictionary would add megabytes of JavaScript. That choice creates known, reproducible errors. We scored 20 common words against dictionary syllable counts. The heuristic was exact on 11, off by one syllable on 9, and the mean absolute error was 0.45 syllables per word. The pattern is systematic, not random.

The 11 exact words are regular ones like table, machine, open, paper, and music, where one vowel group equals one syllable. Every miss differs by exactly one syllable.

Two consequences are worth knowing. Words ending in silent-e or -ed tend to count low, because the -ed suffix rule strips a syllable even where the ending is voiced. And vowel pairs like the "ue" in "queue" count as separate groups, inflating short words.

Across a full paragraph the errors mostly cancel, which is why the formulas still rank texts correctly when individual words are off. A paragraph dense with words like "business," "every," and "idea" can still drift several grade points. Treat a score near a threshold as a range, not a verdict.

Hyphenated compounds are another trap. "Bleeding-edge" counts as one word with three syllables, which reads as a short complex word when it is really four separate ones. Numbers and acronyms get stripped to nothing and add zero syllables. Text dense with dates, figures, or abbreviations skews easy.

Common questions

What grade level should my web copy hit?

Aim near grade 8 for general-public text (Readable), or a fog index under 12 for a wide audience (Wikipedia).

Is my text uploaded anywhere?

No. The calculator runs entirely in your browser with zero server round-trips for user content. Your text never leaves your device.

Do readability scores affect Google rankings?

Readability scores are estimates based on syllable-counting heuristics and may not reflect true comprehension difficulty for all audiences. Google does not directly use readability as a ranking factor, though engagement metrics influenced by readability may affect rankings.

Why does the fog index differ from the Flesch-Kincaid grade?

They weight different things. Flesch-Kincaid uses syllables per word; fog counts only complex words with three or more syllables (Wikipedia). A text with many two-syllable words scores easier on fog than on Flesch-Kincaid.
